Patient complexity and Patient needs

Author

David Fong

The concept of patient complexity is intuitive to most primary care clinicians, but difficult to define in an agreed precise manner [1]. While complexity is often narrowly linked to an arbitrary - albeit significant - outcome such as hospital admission risk, predominantly based on medical risk factors [2], [3], clinicians typically experience complexity much more broadly. For them, it manifests as consultations that are multi-faceted, time-consuming, shaped by psychological, socio-economic and systemic factors, along with multiple - sometimes problematic - interactions between patient, multiple health care providers and community [4], [5], [6]. Some define complexity as the “gap between an individual’s needs and the capacity of the healthcare system to answer those needs” [7]. Notably, greater complexity often correlates with higher overall healthcare use and increased social support needs [1], [6].

Several reporting tools for general practice electronic medical records (EMRs) measure patient complexity. This is particularly relevant for reporting to Primary Health Networks (PHNs) as part of their Performance and Quality Framework, which includes indicator ‘P12’ for tracking potentially preventable hospitalisation [8].

  • PenCS incorporates a CSIRO developed tool using logistic regression to predict hospitalisation using Australian general practice EMR data. Tool workings and development is well-documented and transparent [9], which the authors are willing to share for non-profit purposes. It may be possible to adapt the tool to other outcomes.

  • POLAR uses the Diversion 2 tool, developed using machine learning techniques with demographic, diagnoses, medication, pathology and measurement data extracted from general practices with GRHANITE and linked with hospital admission data [10], [11].

  • Primary Sense integrates the extensively developed, international and proprietary John Hopkins ACG System [12], [13]. The John Hopkins ACG system has been used to estimate current and likely future care needs in primary care, including more equitable funding of general practices in the NHS [14], [15]. However, its specific application in Primary Sense is limited to assessing the risk of hospital admission. A core feature of John Hopkins ACG is patient segmentation, which groups patients into relatively homogenous groups with similar care needs based on medical and socio-economic characteristics [16], [17], [18] (Figure 1).

Opportunities

The concept of patient segmentation presents significant potential value in primary care settings. When combined with modifiers such as social need, patient segmentation has the capacity to predict and enable the provision of services tailored towards behavioural, clinical or demographic drivers of health over and above predictors based on a single score [16], [18], [19], [20] (Figure 2). The patient segmentation approach has proven adaptable for diverse populations, including in Southeast Asia and British Columbia [21], [22] (Figures 3, 4).

Furthermore, developing predictive models for specific outcomes within patient segments could improve predictive power, as both patient needs and importance of specific risk-factors varies between patient segments [22], [23].

Beyond traditional outcomes like hospital admission, alternative intermediate outcomes could be explored to measure complexity and effectiveness. These could include primary care visit frequency and duration, or the volume of correspondence [5].
